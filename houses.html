The Six Great Houses

A crew handout · October 11, 2026

How a servant learns the houses

Every skaa who serves in Luthadel learns the six Great Houses from the back stairs. Nobody introduces you to a lord. You learn which house keeps its lamps burning past midnight, which cook waters the wine, and which steward counts the spoons twice. This handout is that knowledge, gathered by Hearthlight for its new hires. It holds only what any servant could pick up in a season of listening.

House Ostralle

Ostralle keeps soldiers the way other houses keep silver: polished, counted, and kept where visitors can see them. Its lords are steel-blooded, and the whole household is arranged around that fact. A coin dropped in an Ostralle hallway is never a small thing, so servants learn to keep their hands and their pockets tidy.

Below stairs it runs like a barracks. Mornings start before the ash settles, the stewards are old campaigners, and nobody gets praise, though nobody who does the work is ever struck for it either. The food is plain and the pay is on time.

The house reads a guild writ twice at the door and again a month later. It is not suspicion, only habit. A servant who is early, quiet and exact has nothing to fear in Ostralle. A servant who is interesting does.

House Draubel

Draubel does its fighting in ledgers. The house holds a great many debts, a great many favors, and a talent for making rivals feel they were never really rivals. Its lords are said to carry brass and zinc in the blood, which means they are gentle, patient, and very hard to refuse.

Servants in Draubel learn to distrust their own moods. A calm that arrives too easily, a sudden flush of loyalty, or a fear that fades the moment you stop to think about it can all be natural. In Draubel, they might not be. The counting-house clerks swear it is only the house's good manners. The cooks keep their own counsel.

Draubel trusts the guild's writ more than any other house, and it has done so for years. That trust is worth a great deal to you, and a great deal could be lost with one forged seal found out.

House Marisel

Marisel is the house everyone underestimates and nobody forgets. A generation ago it was a minor name. Now its envoys dine in rooms that did not used to admit them, and people say Marisel always knows who sat where and what was said. Its lords are tin-blooded, with senses sharpened past anything a keyhole could stop.

Servants learn to keep their voices low even in the cellars. Whispers carry in Marisel, and so does a servant's reputation for knowing nothing. The best servants are the ones who seem to have no opinions, which is, of course, what everyone below stairs is trying to look like.

Marisel trusts the guild's writ about as much as Draubel does, and for a similar reason: it has never been disappointed. A house that listens this closely has never heard anything wrong about your papers, so far.

House Halcorr

Halcorr was Mistborn once, and its lords still tell you so within the first quarter hour. The blood has thinned over the generations, but the name has not. The manor is vast, dim, and half closed up. Its portraits are better kept than its roof.

The staff is small and ageing. Pay arrives late, and the dinners are long, with a great many stories about grandfathers. Servants here learn patience and the art of looking busy in empty halls. Few people watch a Halcorr servant, which is its own sort of freedom.

Halcorr hires so few skaa that it barely thinks about writs. A placement here is easy to win and easy to forget, and it draws almost no scrutiny. It is also a quiet corner of the city where very little of the Great Houses' business seems to pass through.

House Corvaine

Corvaine holds a contract that ties it to the Pits of Hathsin, and everything about the house is arranged to remind you of it. The doors are heavy and the locks are heavier. Caravans come and go at odd hours under good escort. Its lords are bronze-blooded, and a Seeker can feel another person's Allomancy across a crowded room.

Servants here do not burn, ever, and they do not test whether anyone is near. The older hands teach the young a flat, still way of standing, as though nothing under their skin could be heard. Corvaine pays well, and it expects that quiet in return.

The house reads a guild writ carefully and takes it at face value, but it watches the servants themselves with cold, steady attention. A good paper is only the beginning of earning trust in Corvaine, and it seldom gets you the rest.

House Sevaline

Sevaline keeps a priest's hours and a clerk's memory. Its lords are devout, and obligators dine at their table so often that a spare place is always laid. The blood runs to copper, and Smokers keep secrets well: whatever happens inside a Sevaline house, very little of it is ever overheard.

Below stairs it is orderly and observant. Prayers come before breakfast. The housekeeper keeps a book of every servant's habits, and the book is read aloud to the obligator when he calls. Servants learn to be pious, punctual and unremarkable, with nothing in their conduct that would make a good story.

Sevaline trusts the guild's writ and watches whoever carries it. They believe in paper, and they believe in watching. A servant who passes both is safe in Sevaline. A servant who passes only one is not.

What every servant knows

The guild's older hands pass these rules to new hires, usually over a late supper at the hall.

  1. Do not be the one who is noticed. Whoever is noticed gets remembered, and whoever is remembered gets asked questions.
  2. Your writ is your name in a noble house. Carry it as if it were true.
  3. Everyone below stairs talks, and someone upstairs always listens. Find out who before you say anything that matters.
  4. A servant out on an errand at dusk is invisible. The mists will hide the rest.
  5. The guildhall remembers every placement. Your Handler remembers you.
  6. When you cannot read a noble's face, look at the floor and wait.
